Around 2600 BCE towns along the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra became cities: baked-brick houses on planned streets, covered drains, standard weights, and a script on seals that has not been deciphered. Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira and Lothal traded from Afghanistan to the Gulf. After about 1900 BCE the large cities emptied, and settlement shifted east towards the Yamuna and south into Gujarat.

The Rigveda, probably composed in the greater Punjab between about 1500 and 1200 BCE and handed down orally for centuries before it was written, is the oldest Indian text that can be read. It describes cattle-herding clans and no cities, and it cannot be securely tied to any excavated site. Over the following centuries iron, Painted Grey Ware villages and then walled towns spread across the Ganga plain. By the fifth century BCE the sixteen great kingdoms and republics of the Buddhist lists, the mahajanapadas, contested the north, and the Buddha and Mahavira taught in their capitals.

The map draws no boundary for this chapter. There is no clear evidence that the Harappans formed a single state, and the Vedic clans and early kingdoms left no record of borders. The pins mark excavated sites; where a pin stands for a region, the event says so.
