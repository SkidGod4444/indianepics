Around 2600 BCE towns along the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra became cities: baked-brick houses on planned streets, covered drains, standard weights, and a script on seals that no one has yet read. Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira and Lothal traded from Afghanistan to the Gulf. After about 1900 BCE the large cities emptied, and settlement shifted east towards the Yamuna and south into Gujarat.

The Rigveda, composed in the greater Punjab in the second half of the second millennium BCE, is the oldest surviving Indian text, but it describes no cities and cannot be tied to any excavated site. Iron, Painted Grey Ware villages and then walled towns spread across the Ganga plain. By the fifth century BCE sixteen great kingdoms and republics, the mahajanapadas, contested the north, and the Buddha and Mahavira taught in their capitals.

The map draws no boundary for this chapter. The Harappans left no evidence of a single state, and the Vedic tribes and early kingdoms left none of borders. The pins mark excavated sites; where a pin stands for a region, the event says so.
