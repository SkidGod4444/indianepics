After the Mauryas, power in the subcontinent was plural and regional: the Satavahanas in the Deccan; Indo-Greeks, Shakas and then the Kushans in the north-west; the Chera, Chola and Pandya kings of the Tamil country. From about 320 CE the Guptas built the largest state of the fourth and fifth centuries, centred on the Ganga plain. The same centuries produced Aryabhata's astronomy, the monastery at Nalanda, the Ajanta paintings under Vakataka patrons and, on most scholars' dating, Kalidasa's poetry. In the seventh century Harsha ruled much of the north from Kannauj while the Chalukyas of Badami and the Pallavas of Kanchi contested the south.

The outline is the Gupta empire near its widest, under Chandragupta II around 400 to 415 CE, after the defeat of the western Kshatrapas brought Malwa and Gujarat under Gupta rule. It is approximate. Samudragupta's inscription on the Allahabad pillar describes rings of kings who were uprooted, captured and released, or made to pay tribute, not a border; the line drawn here is the zone of direct rule and leaves the tributaries outside.
