The Bombay government's revision settlement raised Bardoli taluka's land revenue by about 22 per cent, in a district where cotton prices had fallen. In February 1928 Vallabhbhai Patel agreed to lead a refusal to pay, on condition that the villages hold out to the end. He divided the taluka into camps, ran a daily news-sheet, and had women's meetings addressed as often as men's. The government seized buffaloes and household goods, sold land to outsiders, and brought in Pathan enforcers; villagers locked their houses and kept their cattle indoors, and buyers of seized land were boycotted.

The campaign was taken up in the Bombay legislature, where K. M. Munshi and others resigned their seats, and in the national press. In August the governor conceded an inquiry and the return of seized land. The Maxwell–Broomfield inquiry reported in 1929 and cut the increase to 6.03 per cent.

The women of Bardoli gave Patel the title Sardar, chief. The campaign became the model for Gandhian peasant satyagraha, disciplined, local and sharply limited in its demand.
