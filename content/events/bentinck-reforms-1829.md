Regulation XVII of 4 December 1829 declared the burning or burying alive of widows illegal and punishable in the Bengal Presidency; Madras and Bombay followed in 1830. Rammohun Roy had argued against the practice in print for more than a decade. Orthodox Calcutta appealed to the Privy Council against the ban, which dismissed the appeal in 1832.

On 2 February 1835 Thomas Babington Macaulay, law member of the Governor-General's council, wrote his Minute on Education, dismissing Sanskrit and Arabic learning and proposing to form a class "Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste". Bentinck's resolution of 7 March 1835 directed the Company's education funds to teaching in English, and in 1837 Persian ceased to be the language of the courts.

Both measures are read in two ways: as reforms Indian reformers had asked for, and as a colonial state's claim to remake Indian society. Lata Mani has argued that the sati debate was conducted over the heads of the women it concerned.
