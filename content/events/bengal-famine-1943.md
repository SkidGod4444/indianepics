Japan's conquest of Burma in 1942 cut off Bengal's rice imports. Fearing invasion, the government carried out a denial policy, removing boats and surplus rice from the coastal districts, and on 16 October 1942 a cyclone destroyed much of the winter crop in Midnapore. War spending drove prices up, other provinces blocked grain from leaving, and the government gave priority to feeding Calcutta's war industries. By the summer of 1943 the destitute were dying in Calcutta's streets, and in August the Statesman printed photographs of them while officials still avoided the word famine.

The new Viceroy, Wavell, visited Calcutta in October and ordered the army into relief. Most of the deaths, in 1943 and 1944, were from cholera, malaria and smallpox among the starving, and most were in the countryside; the pin marks Calcutta, where the famine was seen and recorded.

The Famine Inquiry Commission put the toll at about 1.5 million; later demographers estimate between 2.1 and 3 million. Amartya Sen argued that there was enough rice and that wartime prices took it out of reach of labourers and fishermen, and others have since argued that the harvest shortfall was larger than he allowed. How much blame falls on the War Cabinet's limits on grain imports, on the provincial government, and on traders is argued still.
